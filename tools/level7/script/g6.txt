read: Deforestation in the Philippines: Challenges and Solutions
¶ The Philippines, home to two-thirds of the planet’s biodiversity and up to four-fifths of its flora and fauna, continues to face severe deforestation. The nation loses an estimated 47,000 hectares of rainforest annually and has already lost around 60% of its forest cover in the last 80 years. As of 2020, only 24.1% of its 30 million-hectare land area remained forested. With the country experiencing an average of 20 major storms each year due to its location in the typhoon belt, deforestation has worsened soil erosion, landslides, flash floods, food and water insecurity, and the displacement of indigenous tribes.
¶ In 2019, the Department of Environment and Natural Resources (DENR) classified 55 mammals, 184 birds, 53 reptiles, 30 amphibians, and 784 invertebrates as threatened species. Anna Varona of the Haribon Foundation warned that clearing forests destroys habitats, food sources, and breeding environments, increasing the risk of extinction. Much of the forest loss stemmed from indiscriminate logging between 1935 and 1991, while agricultural expansion, mining, urbanization, and infrastructure projects further contributed. Climate-induced disasters, such as Typhoon Haiyan in 2013, which killed more than 6,000 people and decimated 86% of mangrove forests in eastern Philippines, have also accelerated forest degradation.
¶ The country’s population of over 110 million has intensified demand for forest resources. According to the Philippine Master Plan for Climate Resilient Forestry Development, pressure continues to rise for wood, fuelwood, and other forest products. The plan, first formulated in 1990 after a government ban on log and lumber exports, was updated in 2003 and again in 2013 to address climate change impacts. In 2011, two major policies were introduced: a moratorium on tree cutting in natural forests with the creation of an Anti-Illegal Logging Task Force, and the National Greening Program (NGP), which aimed to plant 1.5 billion trees on 1.5 million hectares between 2011 and 2016. Extended to 2028, the NGP now covers 7.1 million hectares, with 1.83 billion seedlings planted on 2.18 million hectares as of 2022.
¶ These interventions have yielded modest gains. Data from the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) show a 0.5% increase in forest cover between 2010 and 2020, averaging 34,900 hectares added annually. However, Varona cautioned that planting exotic trees does not promote Philippine biodiversity. She stressed the importance of prioritizing native species to restore ecosystems and preserve biodiversity. This focus has inspired other initiatives such as BINHI, the greening project of the Energy Development Corporation (EDC), the world’s largest vertically integrated geothermal power producer. Established in 2008, BINHI seeks to rescue, preserve, and propagate native species, with 47% of the country’s indigenous trees already threatened with extinction.
¶ BINHI has conducted tree population surveys and partnered with stakeholders to protect forests. To date, it has assessed 1,470 tree species through Botanic Garden Conservation International’s Global Tree Assessment, identifying 145 priority species. Discoveries include red lauan (Shorea negrosensis), once thought confined to Negros, and Mapilig (Xanthostemon bracteatus), previously found only in Camarines Norte. The program also identified dwindling species such as kaladis narig (Vatica elliptica), where only two trees were found in 2016. Through partnerships, 11 trees were later discovered, bearing fruit during the COVID-19 pandemic.
¶ BINHI also plants trees outside their natural habitats to nurture “mother trees” that will produce viable seeds. Plans for in-situ conservation are underway, planting species back into their native environments. While undoing decades of forest damage will take years, reforestation efforts are making progress. As a result of BINHI’s initiatives, 10 of the 145 priority species have been removed from the list, while 21 were downgraded from “critically endangered.” These efforts highlight the importance of combining science, policy, and community action to protect the Philippines’ forests and biodiversity.

read: Get Ready for Yolanda
¶ If typhoon Yolanda/Haiyan stays on track and does not weaken, it will arrive in the Visayas on Friday with winds stronger than those of super typhoon Ruping.
¶ Those of us who lived or worked in Cebu on Nov. 10-14, 1990 remember Ruping’s tracks well. Its center winds, which spun at 145 to 200 kilometers per hour, left more than 200 dead in Central Visayas alone. The typhoon unmoored a foreign vessel, which then hit the only bridge between Cebu and Mactan. Ruping also cut off power and water service to Cebu from one week to a month.
¶ We remember, too, the resilience shown by the region’s residents, the concerted effort to regain our bearings and fix or replace up to P10.8 billion worth of damaged infrastructure. “The storm scarred Cebu,” a Sun.Star editorial observed then, “but also revealed its strengths.”
¶ Yolanda is scheduled to enter the Philippine area of responsibility on Thursday morning, forcing communities in Bohol and Cebu from the disaster recovery stage back to disaster preparedness.
¶ But unlike the earthquake that struck our communities three weeks ago, we have received and continue to receive warnings about Yolanda, including this: The US Navy’s Joint Typhoon Warning Center expects the typhoon will bring 222-kph winds.
¶ There will be time later to check on the progress we’ve made or failed to make, in enforcing the National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Plan for 2011-2028.
¶ We’ll have many opportunities to reexamine and address “the underlying causes” that leave people vulnerable to disaster. And there will be time (and resources, for we are a resilient and resourceful lot) to “build back better.”
¶ For now, however, let us all focus on preparing ourselves for the typhoon that approaches. Start at home. Prepare a basic family kit.
¶ The US Federal Emergency Management Administration recommends preparing enough ready-to-eat food for three days; a gallon of clean water per person per day; a first aid kit (and maintenance medications, we may add); a transistor radio and flashlight with spare batteries; a whistle to signal for help, if needed; dust masks or a cotton shirt in case there’s a need to filter the air; and important family documents packed in a waterproof, portable container.
¶ “Hazards become disasters only if vulnerable people and resources are exposed to them,” the plan goes. Yes, we are resilient, Cebu. This time let’s also be prepared.
